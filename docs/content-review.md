## Lesson: Tell me about yourself

id: `pitch-basics` · track: communication · topic: pitch · 6 min · reviewed: **true**

How to answer “tell me about yourself” in about a minute, using your own experience.

**By the end you'll be able to:**
- Open with who you are, one recent highlight and what you want next
- Match your answer to what the role asks for
- Back up claims with what you did and what changed

### Idea 1: Present, past, future

id: `pitch-shape`

#### Guess first

An interviewer says, “Tell me about yourself.” Which opening works best?

- I grew up in a small town, and then I went to college where I studied a few different things…
- **✔ I'm a front-end developer with three years of experience building React apps. Most recently I worked on our checkout rebuild, and I'm looking for a team that cares about accessibility.**
- Honestly, I'm not sure where to start. I've done a bit of everything.
- I'm a hard worker and a fast learner.

**Reveal:** Start with who you are now, then a recent highlight, then what you're looking for. That tells the interviewer what you do and where you're headed in about a minute. A life story or a list of traits doesn't.

#### Worked example

> “I'm a front-end developer with three years of experience building React apps. Most recently I worked on a rebuild of our checkout form that cut abandoned checkouts by 18%, and I owned the payment fields. Before that, I helped move our component library to TypeScript, and the whole team still uses it. I'm looking for a team where I can work on accessibility at a bigger scale, which is why this role caught my eye.”

1. Present: who you are and what you do now, in one sentence.
2. Past: one or two relevant things you've done, with a result.
3. Future: what you're looking for, and why it points at this role.

#### Your turn

In a “tell me about yourself” answer, which sentence belongs in the “past” part?

- I'm a developer who loves learning new things.
- **✔ Last year I rebuilt our signup flow, which cut drop-off by 12%.**
- I'd like to grow into a tech lead role.
- I studied computer science from 2016 to 2020.

**Explanation:** The past part is a recent highlight with a result. The first is a trait, the third is about the future, and the fourth is background that rarely needs saying.

#### Another look (after a miss)

1. Think of it as a few short sentences: who you are now, one or two things you've done, and what you want next.
2. It's a headline, not a biography. They can ask for more detail afterwards.
3. Aim for about 30 to 60 seconds, which is roughly 70 to 150 words.

**Model answer:**

**Key points:**
- Starts with who you are now
- Gives one recent highlight with a result
- Ends with what you want next, tied to this role
- Takes about a minute, not five

**Recap line:** Open with who you are now, add one recent highlight with a result, then say what you want next. About a minute.

### Idea 2: Match it to the role

id: `pitch-match`

#### Guess first

The job description asks for “experience with React and accessibility.” You've worked on many projects. What do you lead with?

- All of them, in the order you did them
- **✔ The projects that show React and accessibility**
- Your favourite project, even if it's unrelated
- Only how many years you've worked

**Reveal:** Pick the two or three things the role asks for and talk about the experience that matches them. The interviewer is listening for a fit, not a full history.

#### Worked example

> “The role is about building accessible interfaces in React. At my last job I made our form components keyboard-friendly and tested them with a screen reader, and I fixed 40 issues from an accessibility audit. That's the kind of work I'd like to keep doing here.”

1. Read the description and underline two or three things it asks for.
2. For each one, find something you really did that matches, and say what changed because of it.
3. Use the same words the description uses, so the interviewer can hear the match.

#### Your turn

- **✔ I built a sales dashboard in React that 40 people check every morning.**
- I've worked on a few different things over the years.
- I've always been interested in data.

**Explanation:** It names what you built and who uses it, which matches the ask directly. The others are traits or interests, with nothing the interviewer can check.

#### Another look (after a miss)

1. A job description is a list of what they need. Your job is to show where you already have it.
2. Pick two or three items. More than that and the answer turns into a list.
3. For each one: say what they need, what you did that matches, and what came of it.

**Starter:** The role asks for … and I've …

**Key points:**
- Names two or three things the role asks for
- Matches each one to something you actually did
- Says what changed or what the result was
- Uses the role's own words

**Recap line:** Pick two or three things the role asks for and match each to something you've really done, with a result.

### Idea 3: Show it with a result

id: `pitch-evidence`

#### Guess first

In an interview about your past work, which of these is the strongest thing to say?

- I'm a strong problem solver and a great teammate.
- **✔ I cut our build time from 12 minutes to 4 by caching dependencies, and the whole team now uses it.**
- I worked on the build system.
- I'm passionate about developer tools.

**Reveal:** A specific thing you did, with a number or an outcome, is something the interviewer can picture and check. Words like “strong” and “passionate” are easy to say and hard to believe.

#### Worked example

> Weaker: “I'm good at performance.”
>
> Stronger: “I cut our page load from 4 seconds to 1.5 by compressing images and splitting the bundle, and our bounce rate dropped.”

1. The weaker version is a claim. The stronger one is evidence.
2. Good evidence has three parts: what you did, how, and what changed.
3. If you don't have an exact number, say what changed instead, like “the support tickets about it stopped.” Don't invent a figure.

#### Your turn

In an interview answer about your work, which version is the strongest?

- I improved the app's performance.
- I'm very good at making things fast.
- **✔ I cut the page load from 4s to 1.5s by compressing images.**
- I worked on performance for a while.

**Explanation:** It says what you did, how, and by how much. The others describe the topic without showing a result.

#### Another look (after a miss)

1. A claim says you're good at something. Evidence shows it.
2. Use this shape: what you did, how you did it, what changed.
3. If you don't have a number, describe the change. Never make up a figure: interviewers often ask how you measured it.

**Fresh question 1**

In an interview, you don't have an exact number for a result you achieved. What's the best thing to do?

- Estimate a number that sounds good
- **✔ Describe what changed, for example “the support tickets about it stopped”**
- Skip the result entirely
- Say the result was huge

**Explanation:** A real, describable change is honest and still gives evidence. An invented number can fall apart when the interviewer asks how you measured it.

**Fresh question 2**

In an interview answer, which line is a claim rather than evidence?

- **✔ I'm excellent at debugging.**
- I traced a memory leak to an unclosed event listener and fixed it.
- I reduced crash reports by 30%.
- I added caching, which cut API calls in half.

**Explanation:** “Excellent at debugging” is a claim. The other three say what was done or what changed.

#### Say it

What's something you've built that you're proud of?

**Starter:** I built …, and the result was …

**Model answer:**
> I built a sales dashboard in React that the whole sales team checks each morning. Before it, they pieced numbers together from three spreadsheets, so I pulled them into one view and added filters by region. They told me it saved them about an hour a week, and it's still in use a year later.

**Key points:**
- Says what you built and who uses it
- Says how or why you built it that way
- Gives a result, with a number or a clear outcome
- Only claims what's true and checkable

**Recap line:** Back every claim with what you did and what changed. Never invent a number.

## Lesson: When they dig into your experience

id: `pitch-followups` · track: communication · topic: experience · 6 min · reviewed: **true**

What to do when the interviewer starts digging into your experience.

**By the end you'll be able to:**
- Say clearly what you personally did
- Walk through a project from situation to result
- Answer honestly about gaps and mistakes

### Idea 1: Say what you did

id: `own-your-part`

#### Guess first

The interviewer asks, “What was your part in that project?” Which answer is better?

- We built it as a team and it went really well.
- **✔ Three of us built it. I owned the search feature: I designed the API and built the screen.**
- I did all of it myself.
- It was mostly the other developers.

**Reveal:** Say what you personally owned and credit the team honestly. “We” on its own hides your part, and claiming everything usually falls apart under the next question.

#### Worked example

> “Three of us rebuilt the checkout form. My part was the payment form: I wrote the validation, set up the error messages and tested it with a screen reader. Another developer owned the API, and a designer handled the layout.”

1. Name the team size, so the scale is clear.
2. Say what you owned, using “I”.
3. Credit the rest with “we” or by role. It sounds confident and honest.

#### Your turn

Which answer is best when asked, “What was your role?”

- We all worked on everything.
- I led the whole project.
- **✔ I owned the payment form, and worked with another developer and a designer on the rest.**
- I helped out where I could.

**Explanation:** It's specific about your part and honest about the team. “We all worked on everything” hides your part, and “helped out” undersells it.

#### Another look (after a miss)

1. Interviewers ask this to find out what you personally did, not what the team did.
2. Use “I” for your own work and “we” for shared work.
3. Be specific: a feature, a fix or a decision, not a general sense of having been involved.

**Fresh question 1**

In an interview about a team project, you're asked what your part was. Which phrase is the weakest start?

- I owned the search screen.
- **✔ We did a bit of everything.**
- I wrote the API for it.
- I led the testing.

**Explanation:** “We did a bit of everything” doesn't tell the interviewer what you did. Starting with something specific you owned does.

**Fresh question 2**

In an interview, if someone else did most of a project you mention, what should you say?

- Take credit for it, since you were there
- **✔ Say clearly what you did, and credit the others**
- Say nothing about the team
- Say you weren't involved

**Explanation:** Being honest about your part is more convincing than a bigger claim, and the next question usually checks it.

#### Say it

What was your specific part in that project?

**Starter:** There were … of us. I owned …

**Model answer:**
> There were three of us. I owned the payment form: I wrote the validation, designed the error messages and tested it with a screen reader. Another developer owned the API and a designer handled the layout, so I worked closely with both.

**Key points:**
- Gives the team size
- Says what you personally owned, using “I”
- Credits others honestly
- Names a concrete piece of work

**Recap line:** Say what you personally owned. Use “I” for that and “we” for the rest.

### Idea 2: Walk me through a project

id: `walk-through`

#### Guess first

The interviewer says, “Walk me through a project you worked on.” What order works best?

- Result first, then jump around
- **✔ Context, your task, what you did, then the result**
- Every technical detail, from the first commit
- Only the technologies you used

**Reveal:** A simple order is: what the situation was, what you needed to do, what you did, and what happened. It's often called STAR: situation, task, action, result.

#### Worked example

> “Our checkout form had a high drop-off rate, and I was one of three people asked to rebuild it. My job was the payment form. I watched session recordings, saw people getting stuck on the card fields, and rebuilt them with better validation and clearer errors. Drop-offs on the whole checkout fell by 18% over the next month.”

1. Situation: the context, in a sentence.
2. Task: what you were responsible for.
3. Action: what you did, with the details that show your skill. Result: what changed.

#### Your turn

In a project walkthrough during an interview, which line is the result?

- Checkout drop-offs were high.
- My job was the payment form.
- I rebuilt the card fields with clearer errors.
- **✔ Drop-offs fell by 18% the following month.**

**Explanation:** The result says what changed afterwards. The first is the situation, the second the task and the third the action.

#### Another look (after a miss)

1. Tell it like a short story with four beats: situation, task, action, result.
2. Spend the most time on the action. That's where your skill shows.
3. Finish with what changed, then stop. They'll ask more if they want it.

**Fresh question 1**

In an interview, which part of a project walkthrough should get the most time?

- The situation
- **✔ The action: what you did**
- The result
- The team's background

**Explanation:** The action is where the interviewer learns how you work, so give it the most detail.

**Fresh question 2**

In an interview, a candidate describes a project: “The app was slow, and my job was to speed it up. I added caching and split the bundle.” Which part is missing?

- The situation
- The task
- The action
- **✔ The result**

**Explanation:** It gives the problem and what you did, but not what changed afterwards. Add the result, such as how much faster it got.

#### Say it

Walk me through a project you're proud of.

**Starter:** The situation was … My job was … I …

**Model answer:**
> Our checkout form had a high drop-off rate, and I was one of three people asked to rebuild it. My job was the payment form. I watched session recordings, saw people getting stuck on the card fields, and rebuilt them with better validation and clearer errors. Drop-offs on the whole checkout fell by 18% over the next month. If I did it again, I'd test with real users earlier.

**Key points:**
- Sets the scene in a sentence
- Says what you were responsible for
- Describes what you did, in some detail
- Ends with a result

**Recap line:** Walk through a project as situation, task, action, result, and spend the most time on the action.

### Idea 3: Gaps and what went wrong

id: `be-honest`

#### Guess first

The interviewer asks, “Have you used GraphQL?” You haven't. What's the best answer?

- Yes, a bit.
- **✔ No, not yet. I've worked with REST APIs from React for three years, so I expect to pick it up quickly.**
- I don't remember.
- No.

**Reveal:** Say honestly that you haven't, then point to the closest thing you have done. It's believable and it shows how you'd handle it on the job. Bluffing is risky because the next question will test it.

#### Worked example

> “I haven't used GraphQL in a job yet. I've worked with REST APIs from React for three years, so I'm used to fetching and shaping data, and last year I picked up Redux on a deadline. I'd start with the official tutorial and build something small.”

1. Say plainly that you haven't done it.
2. Mention the closest related thing you have done.
3. Say how you'd close the gap. Keep it short.

#### Your turn

Asked “What would you do differently on that project?”, which answer is best?

- Nothing, it went perfectly.
- **✔ I'd have tested with real users sooner, because we didn't find two usability issues until after launch.**
- I'd have had a better team.
- I'm not sure.

**Explanation:** A specific, honest lesson shows you reflect and learn. “Nothing” sounds unaware, and blaming others or saying you're unsure gives them nothing to work with.

#### Another look (after a miss)

1. Honesty works better than bluffing: the next question usually checks the claim.
2. For something you haven't done, say so, then share the closest thing you have done and how you'd learn it.
3. For a mistake, say what happened, what you did and what you learned. Keep it specific.

**Fresh question 1**

- **✔ Say you haven't, then mention something related you have done**

**Fresh question 2**

In an interview, what makes an answer about a mistake work?

**Explanation:** A specific mistake, what you did about it and what you learned shows reflection. Blaming others or picking something trivial doesn't.

#### Say it

Tell me about something that didn't go well.

**Starter:** We ran into … I learned …

**Model answer:**
> On the checkout rebuild we shipped a validation rule that rejected some valid billing postcodes, and support tickets went up for a day. I rolled it back, wrote tests for the edge cases we'd missed and added a check to our release process. It taught me to test with messy real-world data, not just clean examples.

**Key points:**
- Describes a real problem plainly
- Says what you did about it
- Names a specific lesson
- Doesn't blame other people

**Recap line:** Be honest about gaps and name the closest thing you've done. Take one specific lesson from a mistake.

## Lesson: Strengths and growth areas

id: `pitch-strengths` · track: communication · topic: strengths · 6 min · reviewed: **true**

How to answer “what are your strengths and weaknesses?” with real moments from your own work.

**By the end you'll be able to:**
- Back up a strength with one real moment and what it changed
- Describe a growth area, what it cost and what you do differently now
- Turn a vague answer into a specific one

### Idea 1: A strength, with a moment

id: `strength-evidence`

#### Guess first

An interviewer asks, “What's one of your strengths?” Which answer works best?

- I'm hard-working, organised, creative, friendly and a fast learner.
- **✔ I check my work the way people will use it. On our checkout rebuild, a screen reader test showed me that error messages weren't being read out, and I fixed that before launch.**
- I'm probably the best at React on any team I join.
- I don't really like talking about myself.

**Reveal:** Name one strength, then show it with a real moment. A list of traits is hard to remember, and a big claim with nothing behind it is hard to believe.

#### Worked example

> “One strength of mine is checking my work the way people will really use it. On our checkout rebuild, I tried the payment form with a keyboard and a screen reader and found that error messages weren't being read out. I fixed it before launch, and the team added that check to our code reviews.”

1. Name it: one strength, in plain words.
2. Evidence: one real moment, including what you did.
3. Change: what was different for the work or the team afterwards.

#### Your turn

“I'm good at explaining things. Last month a new teammate was stuck on how our forms handle errors, so I drew the flow and walked through it with them.” What would make this stronger?

- Adding four more strengths
- Saying “really good” instead of “good”
- **✔ Saying what changed, for example what the teammate could then do**
- Removing the example

**Explanation:** It names a strength and gives a moment, but stops before the effect. What changed for the work or the team is the missing part.

#### Another look (after a miss)

1. Pick one strength that matters for this role. One with proof is easier to remember than a list.
2. Think of a time it showed. Work, a course, a personal project or a job outside tech can all count.
3. Finish with what changed. If you can't point to a number, describe the change. Never invent a number or a result.

**Fresh question 1**

Which part is missing? “I'm very organised, and that has really helped my team.”

- The name of the strength
- **✔ A real moment that shows it**
- A second strength
- Nothing, it's complete

**Explanation:** It names a strength and says it helped, but gives no moment the interviewer can picture or ask about.

**Fresh question 2**

You can't think of a measurable result for your example. What's the best thing to do?

- **✔ Describe what changed, such as “the team started doing the same check”**
- Make up a percentage
- Leave out the example and just name the strength
- Say it saved the company a lot of money

**Explanation:** A real change you can describe is enough. Only claim results that happened, because a follow-up question may ask how you know.

#### Say it

What's one of your strengths? (Use a moment that really happened.)

**Starter:** One strength of mine is … For example, …

**Model answer:**
> One strength of mine is checking my work the way people will really use it. On our checkout rebuild, I tried the payment form with a screen reader and found that error messages weren't being read out. I fixed it before launch, and the team added that check to our code reviews.

**Key points:**
- Names one strength
- Gives one real moment as evidence

## Lesson: Sorting and transforming arrays

id: `arrays-copy-vs-mutate` · track: javascript · topic: arrays · 6 min · reviewed: **true**

Three things that trip people up with arrays in interviews: sort() changing the array you call it on, sorting numbers, and when to use map or filter.

**By the end you'll be able to:**
- Explain why `sort()` changes the original array, and how to avoid that
- Sort numbers correctly with a compare function
- Choose between `map` and `filter`, and say why

### Idea 1: sort() changes the original

id: `sort-mutates`

#### Guess first

In JavaScript, what do you think this prints?

```js
const a = [3, 1, 2];
const b = a.sort();
console.log(a, b === a);
```

- `[3, 1, 2] false`
- **✔ `[1, 2, 3] true`**
- `[1, 2, 3] false`
- `[3, 1, 2] true`

**Reveal:** `sort()` rearranges the original array in place and returns that same array, so `a` is now sorted and `b === a` is `true`.

#### Worked example

```js
const scores = [30, 10, 20];
const sorted = [...scores].sort();
console.log(scores); // [30, 10, 20]
console.log(sorted); // [10, 20, 30]
```

1. `[...scores]` builds a new array with the same items (a shallow copy).
2. `sort()` reorders that copy and returns it.
3. `scores` is untouched, because only the copy was sorted. Calling `scores.sort()` directly would have reordered `scores` itself.

#### Your turn

In JavaScript, fill in the blank so names keeps its original order.

```js

const sortedNames = ___.sort();
```

- `names`
- **✔ `[...names]`**
- `names.length`
- `names.sort`

**Explanation:** `[...names]` makes a copy, so `sort()` only reorders the copy. Using `names` directly would reorder `names` itself.

#### Another look (after a miss)

1. Some array methods change the array you call them on. That's called mutating it, and `sort()` is one of them.
2. Others, like `map` and `filter`, leave the original alone and hand back a new array.
3. When you need the original order later, copy first with `[...arr]` or `arr.slice()`, then sort the copy.

```js
const a = [3, 1, 2];
a.sort();
console.log(a); // [1, 2, 3]  (a itself changed)
```

**Fresh question 1**


- **✔ `"a"`**
- `"c"`
- `undefined`

**Explanation:** `sort()` reordered `list` itself, so `list[0]` is now `"a"`. `result` is just another name for the same array.

**Fresh question 2**


**Explanation:** Only `[...prices].sort()` sorts a copy. The others reorder `prices` itself: `slice()` runs after `sort()` has already changed it.

**Starter:** sort() changes the original array, so if I need to keep the order I…

**Model answer:**
> `sort()` sorts the array in place, which means it changes the original array instead of returning a new one. If I still need the original order, I copy it first, for example with `[...arr].sort()`, and sort the copy. For instance, calling `scores.sort()` would reorder `scores` itself.

**Key points:**
- It changes (mutates) the original array
- It sorts in place and returns that same array
- To keep the original, copy first, e.g. `[...arr].sort()`

**Recap line:** `sort()` changes the array you call it on. Copy first with `[...arr]` when you need the original.

### Idea 2: Sorting numbers

id: `sort-numbers`

#### Guess first

In JavaScript, what do you think this prints?

```js
console.log([5, 25, 100].sort());
```

- `[5, 25, 100]`
- **✔ `[100, 25, 5]`**
- `[25, 5, 100]`
- `[100, 5, 25]`

**Reveal:** With no function, `sort()` compares items as text. As text, `"100"` comes before `"25"`, and `"25"` before `"5"`, so the result is `[100, 25, 5]`.

#### Worked example

```js
const nums = [5, 25, 100];
nums.sort((a, b) => a - b);
console.log(nums); // [5, 25, 100]
```

1. `sort()` can take a compare function. It gets two items, `a` and `b`, and answers: which goes first?
2. A negative result means `a` goes first. Positive means `b` goes first. Zero means leave them as they are.
3. `a - b` is negative when `a` is smaller, so smaller numbers come first. Use `b - a` for largest first.

#### Your turn

In JavaScript, fill in the blank to sort the numbers from largest to smallest.

```js

nums.sort((a, b) => ___);
```

- `a - b`
- **✔ `b - a`**
- `a > b`
- `a + b`

**Explanation:** `b - a` is negative when `a` is the bigger number, so the bigger number goes first. `a - b` does the opposite. `a > b` gives `true` or `false`, not a number, so it doesn't sort reliably.

#### Another look (after a miss)

1. The compare function answers one question: should `a` come before `b`?
2. Negative means yes, `a` first. Positive means no, `b` first. Zero means it doesn't matter.
3. Try `a = 2` and `b = 7`. `a - b` is `-5`, which is negative, so 2 goes first. That's ascending order.

```js
[10, 2, 33].sort((a, b) => a - b); // [2, 10, 33]
```

**Explanation:** Without a compare function the numbers are compared as text: `"100" < "20" < "3"`. So the result is `[100, 20, 3]`.

**Model answer:**
> By default, `sort()` converts the items to strings and compares them as text, so `"10"` comes before `"9"` because `"1"` is smaller than `"9"`. To sort numbers properly I pass a compare function, like `(a, b) => a - b`, which puts smaller numbers first.

**Key points:**

- So `"10"` sorts before `"9"`: it compares characters, not size
- Fix: pass a compare function such as `(a, b) => a - b`

**Recap line:** Default `sort()` compares as text. Pass `(a, b) => a - b` to sort numbers smallest first.

### Idea 3: map and filter

id: `map-filter`

#### Guess first

In JavaScript, what do you think this prints?

```js

console.log(result, nums.length);
```

- **✔ `[3, 4] 4`**
- `[3, 4] 2`
- `[false, false, true, true] 4`
- `[1, 2] 4`

**Reveal:** `filter` keeps the items where the function returns `true` and gives you a new array. `nums` itself isn't changed, so its length is still 4.

#### Worked example

```js
const scores = [30, 10, 20];
const doubled = scores.map(n => n * 2);
const big = scores.filter(n => n > 15);
console.log(doubled); // [60, 20, 40]
console.log(big);     // [30, 20]
```

1. `map` runs the function on every item and collects the results: 60, 20, 40.
2. `filter` runs the function on every item too, but keeps only the items where it returns `true`.
3. Both return new arrays, and `scores` is unchanged.

1. `map` is for changing every item. You get the same number of items out as you put in.
2. `filter` is for choosing items. You get some of the same items back, so maybe fewer.
3. `forEach` just runs code for each item and returns nothing, so it can't build the new array for you.

```js

[1, 2, 3].filter(n => n > 1); // [2, 3]
```

## Lesson: Copying objects

id: `objects-references` · track: javascript · topic: objects · 6 min · reviewed: **true**

Why changing a copy can change the original, and how to make a copy that doesn't.

**By the end you'll be able to:**
- Explain why assigning an object doesn't copy it
- Describe what a shallow copy is and where spread falls short
- Make a deep copy with structuredClone

### Idea 1: Assigning shares the object

id: `shared-reference`

#### Guess first

In JavaScript, what do you think this prints?

```js
const a = { n: 1 };
const b = a;
b.n = 2;
console.log(a.n);
```

- `1`
- **✔ `2`**
- `undefined`
- `It throws an error`

**Reveal:** `b = a` gives both names the same object, so changing `b.n` changes `a.n` too.

#### Worked example

```js
const a = { n: 1 };
const b = a;
b.n = 2;
console.log(a.n);     // 2
console.log(a === b); // true
```

**Diagram:** Two names, one object. The names a and b both point to the same object, where n is 2.

1. An object lives in one place. `a` and `b` are two names that point to it.
2. `const b = a` **doesn't copy anything**. It gives the same object a second name.
3. So changing `b.n` changes what `a` sees too, and `a === b` is `true`.

#### Your turn

In JavaScript, which one gives you a separate object, so changing one doesn't touch the other?

```js
const original = { n: 1 };
const other = ___;
```

- `original`
- **✔ `{ ...original }`**
- `[original]`
- `original.n`

**Explanation:** `{ ...original }` builds a new object with the same properties. Using `original` just adds a second name for the same object.

#### Another look (after a miss)

1. Think of an object as a document in a shared drive. A variable holds the link, not the document.
2. `const b = a` copies the link. Edits through either link change the same document.
3. To get a separate document you have to make a copy on purpose.

```js
const a = { n: 1 };
const b = a;        // same object
const c = { ...a }; // a new object
```

**Fresh question 1**

In JavaScript, what does this print?

```js
const x = { v: 1 };
const y = x;
y.v = 5;
console.log(x.v);
```

- `1`
- **✔ `5`**
- `undefined`
- `It throws an error`

**Explanation:** `y` is another name for the same object as `x`, so changing `y.v` changes `x.v` too.

**Fresh question 2**

In JavaScript, what does this print?

```js
const x = { v: 1 };
const y = { v: 1 };
console.log(x === y);
```

- `true`
- **✔ `false`**
- `undefined`
- `It throws an error`

**Explanation:** `===` on objects asks whether they are the same object, not whether they look the same. These are two separate objects, so it's `false`.

#### Say it

If I write const b = a with an object and then change b, what happens to a, and why?

**Starter:** Both names point to…

**Model answer:**
> `a` changes too. A variable holds a reference to the object, not the object itself, so `const b = a` doesn't copy the object, it just gives it a second name. Both variables point at the same object, so a change made through `b` is visible through `a`. If I want a separate object, I have to copy it, for example with `{ ...a }`.

**Key points:**
- Assigning an object copies the reference, not the object
- Both names point to the same object, so changes show up in both
- To get a separate object you need to copy it, e.g. `{ ...a }`

**Recap line:** Assigning an object to another variable gives both names the same object.

### Idea 2: Spread is a shallow copy

id: `shallow-copy`

#### Guess first

In JavaScript, what do you think this prints?

```js

const copy = { ...user };
copy.tags.push("b");

```

- `["a"]`
- **✔ `["a", "b"]`**

## Lesson: How property lookup works

id: `property-lookup` · track: javascript · topic: objects · 6 min · reviewed: **true**

Where JavaScript looks when you read a property, and what happens when you write one.

**By the end you'll be able to:**
- Explain the prototype chain
- Tell own properties apart from inherited ones

### Idea 1: Reading walks the chain

id: `proto-chain`

#### Guess first

In JavaScript, what does this print?

```js
const base = { greeting: "hi" };
const child = Object.create(base);
console.log(child.greeting);
```

- **✔ `"hi"`**
- `undefined`
- `It throws an error`

**Reveal:** `child` has no `greeting` of its own, so JavaScript looks at its prototype, `base`, and finds it there.

#### Worked example

```js
const base = { greeting: "hi" };
const child = Object.create(base);
console.log(child.greeting); // hi
child.greeting = "yo";
console.log(child.greeting); // yo
console.log(base.greeting); // hi
```

1. A read checks the object itself first, then its prototype, then that one's prototype, until it reaches `null`.
2. A plain assignment doesn't change the prototype. It creates a property on `child` that hides (shadows) the inherited one.
3. A setter or a read-only property higher up the chain changes what an assignment does.

#### Your turn

In JavaScript, what does this print?

```js

```

- `"L"`
- **✔ `"M"`**
- `undefined`

**Explanation:** The assignment created a `size` on `order`. `defaults` was not touched.

#### Another look (after a miss)

1. Picture each object holding a link to a backup object.
2. Reading follows the links. Writing normally stays on the object you wrote to.

```js
const parent = { colour: "red" };
const kid = Object.create(parent);
parent.colour = "blue";
console.log(kid.colour); // blue
```

**Fresh question 1**

In JavaScript, what does this print?

```js

```

- `undefined`
- `It throws an error`
- **✔ `1`**

**Explanation:** The lookup goes from `c` to `b` to `a`, where `n` is found.

**Fresh question 2**

In JavaScript, what does this print?

```js

console.log(point.y);
```

- **✔ `undefined`**
- `null`
- `It throws an error`

**Explanation:** No object in the chain has `y`, so the result is `undefined`.

#### Say it

What happens when you read a property that isn't on the object?

**Starter:** JavaScript first checks the object itself, then …

**Model answer:**
> JavaScript checks the object's own properties first. If it isn't there, it follows the object's prototype and looks there, and keeps going up the chain until it reaches `null`. If nothing has it, the result is `undefined`. Assigning is different: it normally creates a property on the object itself.

**Key points:**
- Own properties are checked first
- Then the prototype chain, ending at `null`
- Missing everywhere gives `undefined`
- Assignment normally creates an own property

**Recap line:** Reads walk the prototype chain. Plain assignment creates an own property.

### Idea 2: Own or inherited?

id: `own-property`

#### Guess first

In JavaScript, what does this print?

```js

```

- **✔ `true`**
- `false`

**Reveal:** `in` checks the whole chain, and an object written with `{}` inherits `toString` from `Object.prototype`.

#### Worked example

```js
const counts = {};
console.log("toString" in counts); // true
console.log(Object.hasOwn(counts, "toString")); // false
const safe = new Map();
console.log(safe.has("toString")); // false
```

1. `in` asks “is it anywhere in the chain?”
2. `Object.hasOwn` asks “is it on this object itself?”
3. A `Map` has no inherited keys, which makes it a good fit when keys come from users.

**Explanation:** The other two also find the inherited `constructor`, even though nobody saved it.

#### Another look (after a miss)

1. A plain object arrives with inherited names such as `toString` and `constructor`.
2. Use `Object.hasOwn` to ignore them, or a `Map` to avoid them.

## Lesson: unknown, any, type and interface

id: `ts-precision` · track: typescript · topic: typescript · 6 min · reviewed: **true**

Two common TypeScript questions, answered with what the compiler actually does.

**By the end you'll be able to:**
- Explain why `unknown` is safer than `any`
- Say what `interface` can do that `type` can't, and the reverse

### Idea 1: unknown makes you check

id: `unknown-any`

#### Guess first

In TypeScript, which line does the compiler reject?

```js
const loose: any = "hi";
const careful: unknown = "hi";
```

- **✔ `careful.toUpperCase()`**
- `loose.toUpperCase()`
- `Both lines`
- `Neither line`

**Reveal:** `any` switches checking off. `unknown` accepts any value but won't let you use it until you've shown what it is.

#### Worked example

```js
function shout(input: unknown): string {
  if (typeof input === "string") {
    return input.toUpperCase();
  }
  return "not text";
}
```

1. Anything can be passed in, because every type fits into `unknown`.
2. Inside the `if`, TypeScript narrows `input` to `string`, so `toUpperCase` is allowed.
3. Use `unknown` for data from outside: parsed JSON, responses, caught errors. These checks happen at run time. A type annotation alone checks nothing at run time.

#### Your turn

1. `any` says “trust me”.
2. `unknown` says “prove it first”.

**Explanation:** The cast disappears when the code is compiled, so bad data gets through.

#### Say it

What's the difference between unknown and any?

**Starter:** Both accept any value, but …

**Model answer:**
> Both accept any value. With `any`, the compiler stops checking: I can call or index it however I like, and it spreads to whatever I assign it to. With `unknown`, I have to narrow it first, using `typeof`, `instanceof`, a type guard or a schema. I use `unknown` for anything coming from outside.

- Both accept any value
- `any` turns checking off and spreads

- Names a way to narrow

**Recap line:** `any` turns checking off. `unknown` makes you narrow before you use the value.

### Idea 2: interface or type

id: `interface-type`

#### Guess first

In TypeScript, what happens with these two declarations?

```js

```

- **✔ `They merge into one Cart`**
- `The second replaces the first`
- `It is a duplicate name error`

**Reveal:** Interfaces with the same name in the same scope merge. Two type aliases with the same name are an error.

#### Worked example

```js

interface Product { price: number }
const mug: Product = { name: "Mug", price: 12 };

type Size = "S" | "M" | "L";
```

1. Both `Product` declarations count, so `mug` needs `name` and `price`. This is how you can add to a library's interface.
2. `Size` is a union. An interface can't express that; `type` can, along with tuples, mapped and conditional types.
3. Both can describe an object shape, be built on (`extends` for `interface`, `&` for `type`) and be used with `implements`. A class can't implement a union.

**Starter:** Both can describe an object. The differences are …

**Model answer:**
> Both can describe object shapes. Interfaces with the same name merge, which lets consumers add to a library's types. Type aliases can't merge, but they can name unions, tuples and computed types. I use `interface` for object contracts others may extend, and `type` for unions and composed types, and stay consistent with the codebase.

**Key points:**
- Both describe object shapes
- Interfaces merge, `type` aliases don't
- `type` can express unions and tuples
- Gives a rule you actually follow

**Recap line:** `interface` merges. `type` names unions, tuples and computed types.

## Lesson: Where do the rules live?

id: `where-rules-live` · track: engineering · topic: design · 6 min · reviewed: **true**

How to explain which checks belong in the browser, on the server and in the database.

**By the end you'll be able to:**
- Say why the server must work out prices itself
- Choose rules the database should enforce

### Idea 1: The server decides

id: `server-decides`

#### Guess first

In an online shop, the page sends the server the item and the total price when you pay. What's the problem?

- **✔ `A user can change the price before it's sent`**
- `The request is too large`
- `Prices can't be sent as numbers`
- `There is no problem`

**Reveal:** Anything sent from the browser can be edited. The server should receive the choices and work out the price itself.

#### Worked example

```js
const prices: Record<string, number> = { mug: 1200, shirt: 2500 };

// Runs on the server. Amounts are in cents.
function priceFor(item: string, quantity: number): number {
  if (!Object.hasOwn(prices, item)) throw new Error("unknown item");
  if (!Number.isInteger(quantity) || quantity < 1) throw new Error("bad quantity");
  return prices[item] * quantity;
}
```

1. The browser sends only the item and quantity. The price comes from data the server controls.
2. The server checks both inputs, even if the page already did.
3. Browser checks are for quick feedback. Server checks are for safety.
4. A pure function like this is easy to unit test.

#### Worked example


1. `not null` and `check` refuse bad values.
2. `references` refuses a line that points to an order that doesn't exist.
3. `unique` stops the same product appearing twice in one order, even with requests arriving together.
4. The server should catch the error and reply with a clear message.

### Idea 2: Wait for the result, not the clock

id: `test-waiting`

#### Guess first

In a browser test of a checkout page, the test sleeps for 3 seconds before looking for “Order placed”. What can go wrong?

- `Nothing, 3 seconds is always enough`
- **✔ `It fails on a slow run and wastes time on a fast one`**
- `The page reloads`

**Reveal:** A fixed wait is a guess about speed. Waiting for the text itself adapts to each run.

#### Worked example

```js
import { test, expect } from "@playwright/test";

test("places an order", async ({ page }) => {
  await page.goto("/checkout");
  await page.getByRole("button", { name: "Pay" }).click();
  await expect(page.getByText("Order placed")).toBeVisible();
});
```

1. `getByRole` finds the button the way a user would, so a styling change doesn't break the test.
2. `click` waits until the button is ready to be clicked.
3. This `expect` retries until the text shows or the time limit passes. No sleep is needed.
4. For failures in CI, keep a trace so you can see what happened.
